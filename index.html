Part A

Foundations: see the child, then the number

Units 1–3 · ~4 hours

Every device in this module exists to fix one of two problems: getting oxygen into the blood, or getting carbon dioxide out. Part A teaches you to tell them apart at the bedside — and to notice when a reassuring number is hiding a failing child. It is open from the first minute.

▸Unit 1 Start with the child Not attempted

A deteriorating child needs support while the cause is found. The first skill in respiratory care is not choosing a device — it is describing what you see so clearly that the next person knows what to do.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Describe a breathing child by alertness, effort, air entry, perfusion and the reliability of any monitor reading, before naming a diagnosis.
  • UG Make a treatment plan testable: an action, an expected response, a named responder and a reassessment point.
  • UG/PG Call for help in a structure that gets the right person, with the right equipment, quickly.
  • PG Explain what an online assessment can and cannot show about procedural competence.
Your role
  • Student. Describe what you see before naming a diagnosis. State the next supervised action and why.
  • Ward nurse. Communicate a change from baseline, the current support and the help you need; use closed-loop confirmation.
  • PICU nurse. Connect the patient trend to device delivery and team response. Identify which observation would change the plan.
  • PG resident. Make the physiological problem explicit, assign immediate actions and define when you will reassess.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Begin with a description Must-know

Before choosing a device, describe five things: alertness, respiratory effort, air entry, pulse and perfusion, and how reliable the monitor reading is. A diagnosis can guide treatment, but a child who is getting worse needs support while the cause is investigated.

Compare “the saturation is 96%” with “96% on 2 L/min, but he is drowsier and breathing more shallowly than an hour ago”. The second sentence exposes a possible failure of ventilation that the first one hides.

Pearl

Always say what support the number was measured on. 96% in room air and 96% on a non-rebreathing mask are different children.

2 · Make the plan testable Must-know

State the concern, take an action within your role, then look for a specific response. “Will reassess” is weaker than “I have started oxygen at 1 L/min by prongs; I expect SpO₂ at or above 92% and less recession within 15 minutes; if not, I will call the registrar.” Any deterioration triggers review immediately, not at the planned time.

A testable plan
  CONCERN    what is threatened: oxygenation, ventilation, or both?
  ACTION     what you will do, within your role
  EXPECTED   what improvement should look like, and by when
  OWNER      who reassesses, and who responds if it fails
  FAILURE    the finding that makes you escalate NOW

3 · Ask for help that arrives useful Must-know

A ward nurse finds a previously alert child hard to rouse on oxygen. A useful call gives who and where, the worsening trend, current support, what has been done and what help is needed. The receiver repeats back the urgent action and says who is coming.

Ideal setting
  • A rapid response or PICU outreach team with a defined call number and a response time
  • The caller stays with the child and continues support while help arrives
Resource-constrained setting
  • Early escalation also buys time to arrange oxygen for the journey, monitoring, an escort and a receiving team
  • The local clinician remains responsible for immediate stabilisation while transfer is organised
  • Write the call-out number for the referral centre on the oxygen trolley, not in a drawer

4 · What an online result means — and does not Good-to-know UG/PG

A correct first answer is evidence of what you could decide before feedback. A correct repeat after an explanation is evidence of learning with help. Both are useful; neither demonstrates hands-on competence. Bag-mask ventilation, CPAP set-up and airway skills need observation and feedback from a trained assessor on a mannequin and then at the bedside.

Why you rate your confidence

Confidence matters here. Being very sure of a wrong decision is the most dangerous pattern in respiratory care, which is why this module asks how sure you are before every checkpoint and flags “confident and wrong”.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

A 3-year-old with pneumonia has SpO₂ 95% on 2 L/min nasal prongs. An hour ago she was talking; now she is sleepy, her breathing is shallower and her respiratory rate has fallen from 52 to 30/min. The handover says “sats fine on 2 litres”. What is the most accurate description of her state?

Nudge

What does oxygen do to the saturation of a child who is breathing less?

Structured hint

Put the three changes together — sleepier, shallower, slower — and ask whether a normal SpO₂ on oxygen can exclude a rising CO₂.

Why the answer is what it is

Drowsiness with shallower, slower breathing after a period of distress suggests exhaustion and hypoventilation. Supplemental oxygen can keep the saturation normal while CO₂ rises, so the number is falsely reassuring. She needs urgent senior review, airway and breathing support as needed, and a CO₂ or blood-gas assessment.

B — a falling rate with falling consciousness is loss of compensation, not recovery. C — attributing a change in consciousness to tiredness is the classic error. D — she is not hypoxaemic; more oxygen treats the wrong problem and delays escalation.
Always hand over the child's trend and the support she is on, not only the best number of the shift.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

Which of these plans for a 10-month-old with bronchiolitis started on oxygen is the most testable?

Nudge

Which plan tells the next person exactly what success looks like and what to do if it does not happen?

Structured hint

Look for four parts: an action, a target, a named person and time to reassess, and a failure trigger.

Why the answer is what it is

A testable plan has an action, an expected response, an owner and time for reassessment, and a failure trigger. Only option C has all four.

A — no target, no time, no failure plan. B — “sats up” has no number and no escalation. D — a fixed high flow without reassessment can hide deterioration and wastes oxygen.
If the night nurse cannot tell from your plan when to call you, it is not a plan.

▸Unit 2 Recognise distress and failure Not attempted

Respiratory distress is a child working hard and winning. Respiratory failure is a child working hard and losing — or no longer able to work at all. The difference is often quiet.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise signs of increased work of breathing and interpret the respiratory rate for age.
  • UG Recognise the signs of respiratory failure, including the tiring child whose rate is falling.
  • UG/PG Localise the problem — upper airway, lower airway, lung tissue, or control of breathing — without letting it delay support.
  • PG Recognise mixed failure: hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and circulatory failure together.
Your role
  • Student. Describe the pattern and the physiological threat separately, and justify the urgency of your review.
  • Ward nurse. Escalate falling consciousness, weaker effort or worse air entry even if an early-warning score looks reassuring.
  • PICU nurse. Compare support needs, waveform quality and examination; recognise deterioration before profound desaturation.
  • PG resident. State whether oxygenation, ventilation or both are threatened, and coordinate support with cause-directed evaluation.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Distress can still be compensation Must-know

Fast breathing, recession, nasal flaring, head bobbing and grunting show increased work. An alert child with good air entry may still be maintaining gas exchange — but distress can progress. Interpret the rate against age, temperature, pain and the child's own baseline.

AgeFast breathing (WHO/IMNCI)
Under 2 months60/min or more
2–11 months50/min or more
1–5 years40/min or more

Do not reduce severity to one sign. Ask whether the effort is effective: is air moving, can the child interact or feed, is circulation maintained? Reassess after every intervention and record the direction of change.

2 · Less noise is not improvement Must-know

The tiring child

A previously distressed child who becomes quiet, weak, drowsy or apnoeic may be exhausting. Poor air entry, reduced effort and bradycardia are dangerous findings. A normal saturation on oxygen cannot rule this out. Call skilled help and support breathing now — do not wait for a blood gas to prove failure.

Contrast true recovery: easier breathing, better interaction and feeding, better air entry, stable circulation and less support needed.

3 · Locate the pattern without over-claiming Must-know

PatternCluesThink of
Upper airwayInspiratory stridor, hoarse voice, drooling, sniffing postureCroup, foreign body, anaphylaxis (Unit 15)
Lower airwayWheeze, prolonged expirationBronchiolitis (Unit 12), asthma (Unit 14)
Lung tissueCrackles, grunting, hypoxaemiaPneumonia (Unit 13), PARDS (Unit 16)
Control of breathingIrregular, shallow or absent breathing with little effortRaised intracranial pressure, poisoning, seizures, neuromuscular weakness
The error clinicians actually make

The silent chest. In severe asthma a nearly silent chest means very little air is moving — not that the bronchospasm has resolved. Grunting is an attempt to hold lung volume open. Read every sign in the whole-child picture, and never let localisation delay stabilisation.

4 · Mixed failure Good-to-know UG/PG

Hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and circulatory failure often coexist. A child can need oxygen, ventilation support and treatment of poor perfusion at the same time. When the response to one intervention is incomplete, ask which other mechanism is still unaddressed.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

A 4-year-old with severe wheeze was alert with RR 48/min and marked recession. Thirty minutes later RR is 24/min, she is drowsy, her speech is weaker and air entry is reduced. SpO₂ is 93% on a non-rebreathing mask. What is happening?

Nudge

What else changed at the same time as the respiratory rate?

Structured hint

Recovery brings easier breathing and better interaction. Here consciousness and air entry fell with the rate. What does that combination mean?

Why the answer is what it is

A falling rate with falling consciousness, weaker speech and reduced air entry is loss of compensation — exhaustion. Call for help, support breathing and prepare escalation. Hand over the paired observations so “RR improved” is not mistaken for recovery.

A — the rate fell for the wrong reason. B — reduced air entry is a quieter chest, which in severe asthma is a danger sign. C — salbutamol causes tachycardia and tremor, not drowsiness with poor air entry.
When a struggling child goes quiet, find out why before you relax.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 7-month-old has noisy breathing. Which finding most strongly suggests the problem is in the upper airway?

Nudge

Which phase of breathing makes noise when the narrowing is above the chest?

Structured hint

Upper-airway narrowing is worst when the airway is sucked in during inspiration. Which option describes an inspiratory noise?

Why the answer is what it is

Inspiratory stridor with a hoarse cry points to narrowing at or near the larynx — an upper-airway pattern.

A — crackles suggest lung-tissue disease. B — expiratory wheeze suggests lower-airway obstruction. D — irregular breathing with little effort suggests a problem with the control of breathing.
Localise, then support. The pattern tells you which device and which danger to expect.

▸Unit 3 Oxygenation is not ventilation Not attempted

Oxygenation puts oxygen into blood; ventilation takes carbon dioxide out. They fail for different reasons, respond to different treatments, and — most dangerously — a pulse oximeter only watches one of them.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Explain the difference between oxygenation and ventilation, and why supplemental oxygen can hide hypoventilation.
  • UG Explain why saturation is a proportion, and why an anaemic or shocked child can be well saturated but poorly oxygenated.
  • UG/PG Name the four mechanisms of hypoxaemia and predict which respond to oxygen.
  • PG Use minute and alveolar ventilation to explain why rapid shallow breathing clears little CO₂.
Your role
  • Student. Explain the oxygen-delivery chain from lungs to haemoglobin to cardiac output.
  • Ward nurse. Recognise when a satisfactory saturation does not match the child's state, and request review.
  • PICU nurse. Integrate blood-gas and CO₂ trends with support, perfusion and oxygenation.
  • PG resident. Identify the mechanism limiting delivery or ventilation and choose the next investigation without delaying support.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Two different jobs Must-know

Oxygenation transfers oxygen into blood. Ventilation clears carbon dioxide. Alveolar ventilation depends on useful gas reaching gas-exchanging lung, not on the number of breaths. Rapid shallow breaths waste much of each breath in dead space.

Supplemental oxygen can maintain SpO₂ despite falling ventilation. If breathing is shallow or consciousness worsens, ask about CO₂ clearance: a normal saturation cannot answer that question.

Minute and alveolar ventilation
  minute ventilation   = tidal volume × rate
  alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume − dead space) × rate

  Child A: 150 mL × 20/min = 3.0 L/min   dead space 50 mL
           alveolar = (150 − 50) × 20 = 2.0 L/min
  Child B:  75 mL × 40/min = 3.0 L/min   dead space 50 mL
           alveolar = (75 − 50) × 40 = 1.0 L/min
  Same minute ventilation. Child B clears half the CO2.

2 · Saturation is a proportion, not a quantity Must-know

Most oxygen in blood is carried on haemoglobin. Saturation is the proportion of that carrier which is occupied. A severely anaemic child can have 98% saturation and still too little oxygen content. Tissue delivery also depends on cardiac output: well-saturated blood that does not flow does not oxygenate organs.

Three children, all SpO₂ 98%What is really happening
Comfortable, in room airWell
Severe anaemia (Hb 4 g/dL), poor perfusionLow oxygen content and delivery — needs blood and circulatory support
Drowsy, shallow breathing, on oxygenPossible hypoventilation masked by oxygen — needs CO₂ assessment and breathing support

3 · Why the same oxygen has different effects Must-know UG/PG

MechanismExampleResponds to more oxygen?
Low inspired oxygenAltitudeYes
HypoventilationOpioid, exhaustion, neuromuscular weaknessSaturation improves, but CO₂ keeps rising — the real treatment is ventilation
Ventilation/perfusion mismatchPneumonia, bronchiolitis, asthmaUsually yes
ShuntCollapsed or flooded lung, cyanotic heart diseasePoorly — needs recruitment (PEEP/CPAP) or treatment of the cause
Pearl

If a child needs more and more oxygen for less and less effect, think shunt: the lung needs opening (CPAP, PEEP), not just a higher FiO₂.

4 · The curve is a warning about reserve Good-to-know

The oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve is flat at high oxygen tensions and steep lower down. Once a child reaches the steep part, a small fall in oxygen tension produces a fast fall in saturation. That is why a child “holding” at 90–92% can desaturate abruptly — watch trends and reserve, not a single number. The curve's position shifts with temperature, pH and CO₂; it is not a calculator for converting an unreliable SpO₂ reading.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

Three children each have SpO₂ 98%. Which one is most likely to have inadequate oxygen delivery to the tissues despite this reading?

Nudge

What else, apart from saturation, determines how much oxygen reaches the organs?

Structured hint

Oxygen delivery depends on saturation, haemoglobin and cardiac output. Which child is short of the other two?

Why the answer is what it is

Oxygen delivery depends on saturation × haemoglobin × cardiac output. The child with Hb 4 g/dL and poor perfusion has a normal saturation but little carrier and little flow — tissue oxygen delivery is poor.

B, C and D — each has adequate haemoglobin and circulation; the saturation reflects their state reasonably.
A saturation tells you how full the lorries are, not how many lorries there are or whether they are moving.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 9-year-old with Guillain–Barré syndrome has shallow breathing. On 2 L/min oxygen his SpO₂ is 97%, but he is becoming drowsy. What is the main danger the saturation is not showing?

Nudge

Which respiratory job fails first when the muscles are weak?

Structured hint

Oxygen can keep the saturation normal. What gas builds up when breathing is shallow, and what does it do to consciousness?

Why the answer is what it is

Weak, shallow breathing causes hypoventilation: CO₂ rises and consciousness falls, while supplemental oxygen keeps the saturation normal. He needs CO₂ assessment and ventilatory support, not reassurance from the oximeter.

A — his saturation is normal on oxygen; hypoxaemia is not the problem shown. B — 2 L/min is not toxic; the danger is what the oxygen hides. C — nothing suggests anaemia.
In neuromuscular weakness, watch the effort and the CO₂, not the saturation.

Measure and give oxygen

Units 4–7 · ~5 hours

Oxygen is the most commonly prescribed drug in paediatrics and the least often prescribed properly. This Part covers the measurement you rely on, the prescription you write, the supply chain behind it, and the breath you give when oxygen alone is not enough.

▸Unit 4 Pulse oximetry: trust the child, check the signal Not attempted

The pulse oximeter is the most useful monitor in paediatric respiratory care, and it lies in predictable ways. Before you act on a number, make sure it belongs to the child.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Check that a pulse-oximeter reading is valid: probe, site, waveform and pulse agreement.
  • UG Name the common causes of a misleading reading, including poor perfusion, movement and skin pigmentation.
  • UG/PG State what SpO₂ does not measure, and choose the test that answers the question you actually have.
  • PG Interpret a saturation trend together with the support the child was receiving.
Your role
  • Student. Explain which parts of the reading are reliable before interpreting the number.
  • Ward nurse. Use the right probe and site, compare pulses, reassess perfusion and document the response to correction.
  • PICU nurse. Integrate signal quality with gases and CO₂ trends; recognise changing device and physiological limits.
  • PG resident. Resolve discordance with clinical assessment and the right confirmatory test; never anchor on one reassuring number.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Check that the signal belongs to the child Must-know

  • Use a probe made for the child's size and site; align it and secure it without constriction.
  • Look for a stable waveform (plethysmograph).
  • Compare the displayed pulse with the clinical pulse or ECG. A displayed pulse of 45/min when the child's pulse is 150/min means the saturation cannot be trusted.
Treat the child, not the probe

Assess and support the child while a colleague fixes the signal. Never spend minutes hunting for a perfect reading before treating danger signs.

2 · Why readings mislead Must-know

CauseEffect
Movement, cryingErratic readings, poor waveform
Cold skin, poor perfusion, shockWeak or absent signal; falsely low or unobtainable
Wrong probe size, bright ambient lightUnreliable values
Darker skin pigmentationPulse oximeters can overestimate saturation, so hypoxaemia may be missed
Carbon monoxide, methaemoglobinaemiaStandard oximetry can be misleading; laboratory co-oximetry is needed
Skin pigmentation and occult hypoxaemia

Regulators including the US FDA have highlighted reduced accuracy of pulse oximeters in people with darker skin pigmentation, with a risk of occult hypoxaemia — a true saturation lower than displayed. Do not apply an invented numerical correction. When the examination and the number disagree, believe the examination, improve the measurement conditions, and seek additional assessment.

3 · What the monitor does not measure Must-know

SpO₂ estimates arterial oxygen saturation. It does not measure CO₂, haemoglobin concentration or cardiac output. When ventilation is the question, assess effort, consciousness and CO₂ (blood gas or capnography). When perfusion is the concern, examine the circulation. Choose the test that answers your question rather than ordering everything.

Pearl

A trend needs its context. A stable saturation on steadily increasing oxygen is a worsening child. Chart the support alongside every saturation so the next person can read the trend correctly.

4 · Across settings Must-know

Ideal setting
  • Continuous oximetry with waveform on every child receiving respiratory support; capnography for ventilated children
  • Blood gases available at the bedside
Resource-constrained setting
  • A handheld paediatric pulse oximeter at every triage point and ward is the single most important monitoring purchase
  • Spot-checks at defined intervals, with a written escalation threshold, when continuous monitoring is not available
  • Keep neonatal and paediatric probes, spare batteries and a charging routine; broken probes are the commonest cause of “no oximeter”
India lens

Studies in Indian primary-care settings have found hypoxaemia to be common among sick under-fives and often missed without oximetry. Integrating pulse oximetry into IMNCI assessment is feasible and acceptable to health workers when devices, probes and training are supplied. Hypoxaemia predicts death in childhood pneumonia more strongly than any single clinical sign.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

A shocked, cold toddler has SpO₂ 74% on the monitor, no clear waveform and a displayed pulse of 42/min while his palpable pulse is 150/min. He has fast breathing and recession. What should you do?

Nudge

Is the number reliable? And does an unreliable number mean the child is well?

Structured hint

The pulse mismatch tells you the signal is bad. His examination still shows shock and respiratory distress. What do you do with each?

Why the answer is what it is

The signal is unreliable (no waveform, pulse mismatch), but the child is clearly unwell. Treat the child — oxygen, breathing and circulatory support — while someone improves the measurement. Then reassess with a valid signal.

A — waiting for a good number delays treatment of an obviously sick child. B — an unreliable number is not a basis for intubation on its own. D — there is no valid correction factor; inventing one is dangerous.
A bad signal changes how much you trust the number, not how sick the child is.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 2-year-old with dark skin and pneumonia has SpO₂ 93% on room air with a good waveform, but she has marked recession, grunting and is less interactive than an hour ago. What is the best interpretation?

Nudge

When the number and the child disagree, which do you believe?

Structured hint

Oximeters can overestimate saturation in darker skin. Her examination is worsening. Which option acts on both facts without inventing a correction?

Why the answer is what it is

Pulse oximeters can overestimate saturation in darker skin, and her examination is worsening. Believe the child: give oxygen, review urgently, and confirm with a blood gas where available.

A — ignores both the device limitation and the clinical signs. C — no validated correction exists, and the direction of error would make things worse if applied the wrong way. D — grunting signals significant lung disease.
The oximeter is an assistant. The examination is the senior.

▸Unit 5 Prescribe and reassess oxygen Not attempted

“Give oxygen” is not a prescription. A prescription says why, to what target, by which device, starting where, reassessed when, and what happens if it fails.

  • Ward nurse. Check the prescribed target, source, tubing and interface; document the response and escalate rising needs.

1 · High-flow is a system, not a higher wall flow Must-know

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) delivers heated, humidified gas at high flow through a designed circuit and cannula. The flow meets the child's inspiratory demand, washes CO₂ out of the nasopharynx and gives a modest, variable distending pressure. FiO₂ is titrated separately to the oxygenation target. Ordinary prongs turned up on a wall flowmeter are not high-flow — dry, cold gas at high flow damages the nose and is poorly tolerated.

The cannula should occupy about half the nostril, leaving a leak. Neither the flow display nor a comfortable child proves that ventilation is adequate.

2 · What the trials show Must-know

Read the trials before you reach for the device

PARIS (Franklin et al., NEJM 2018): in infants under 12 months with bronchiolitis and hypoxaemia on general wards, high-flow reduced the need for escalation of care compared with standard oxygen, without reducing ICU length of stay or ventilation. A common reading: high-flow is a useful rescue for infants failing standard oxygen.

PARIS 2 (Franklin et al., JAMA 2023): in 1,567 children aged 1–4 years with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, early high-flow compared with standard oxygen lengthened hospital stay (median about 1.8 vs 1.5 days), prolonged oxygen therapy, and was associated with more ICU admissions (about 13% vs 7%).

The Australasian bronchiolitis guideline (2025): do not use high-flow routinely in infants with mild or moderate bronchiolitis who are not hypoxaemic; reserve it for infants who fail low-flow oxygen or who have severe disease, before CPAP.

The error clinicians actually make

Indication creep. Starting high-flow in a mildly hypoxaemic child because it is available — and then keeping them on it, in hospital, longer. Start with standard oxygen; move to high-flow when standard oxygen is failing.

3 · What the trials show Must-know

Read the positive and negative trials together

Bangladesh (Chisti et al., Lancet 2015): in children under five with severe pneumonia and hypoxaemia in a hospital with close physician monitoring, bubble CPAP reduced treatment failure and deaths compared with standard low-flow oxygen.

Malawi (McCollum et al., Lancet Respiratory Medicine 2019): in district hospitals where CPAP was run mostly by non-physician staff with limited monitoring, children with severe pneumonia given bubble CPAP had higher mortality than those given standard oxygen (the difference was not conventionally significant, but the direction was harmful), and the trial was stopped.

Ethiopia (Lancet Global Health 2024): Gebre et al. randomised 12 general hospitals (1240 children aged 1–59 months with severe pneumonia and hypoxaemia) to locally made bubble CPAP or low-flow oxygen. Care was given in a dedicated corner in front of the nursing station, supervised by general practitioners and paediatricians. Treatment failure was lower with bubble CPAP (0.8% vs 3.4%; adjusted RR 0.24), and all six deaths occurred in the low-flow group. The authors call for implementation research in higher-mortality settings.

How to reconcile them. Bubble CPAP is not a device; it is a device plus trained staff, close monitoring, reliable oxygen, and a plan when it fails. Where those exist it can prevent intubation and death. Where they do not, it can delay recognition of a child who needs more — with fatal results.

India lens

Bubble CPAP is widely used in Indian SNCUs for newborns and increasingly in paediatric wards for bronchiolitis and pneumonia. Before extending it to older children on a general ward, check the conditions that made it work in Bangladesh: trained nurses on every shift, hourly monitoring, a doctor who can respond within minutes, and a transfer route. If those are missing, fix them first.

Prolonged NIV in a failing child makes eventual intubation more dangerous: the child arrives exhausted, acidotic and hypoxaemic. Set a review time (often within 1–2 hours) and a clear failure definition before you start.

Protect the skin at pressure points, manage secretions, vent the stomach, and make a feeding plan. Agree how the child can signal discomfort.

The error clinicians actually make

Two capnography traps. In cardiac arrest or very low pulmonary blood flow, exhaled CO₂ can be absent even with a correctly placed tube. And a positive CO₂ trace does not prove the right depth: a tube in the right main bronchus still shows CO₂. Listen to both sides.

2 · Hypoxaemia: find it, treat it Must-know

Hypoxaemia is the strongest predictor of death in childhood pneumonia, and clinical signs miss a large proportion of it. Every child with pneumonia needs a saturation check, at triage and repeatedly. Give oxygen for SpO₂ below 90% (Unit 5), titrate, and wean when stable above 90% in room air.

The error clinicians actually make

Treating a child with chest indrawing as “severe” by reflex and admitting for injections — while never measuring the saturation. The 2024 guideline moved chest indrawing without danger signs to outpatient oral amoxicillin; the decision that matters more is whether the child is hypoxaemic.

3 · When oxygen is not enough Must-know UG/PG

Persistent severe distress, grunting and rising oxygen needs mean the child may benefit from bubble CPAP (Unit 9) where a monitored system exists, or from ventilation. Look for complications that change the plan: pleural effusion or empyema (dull percussion, reduced air entry, persisting fever — ultrasound and drainage), septic shock (Sick Child module), pneumothorax, and heart failure (big liver, gallop — consider congenital heart disease).

Ideal setting
  • Chest X-ray and ultrasound for complications; blood culture; PICU for respiratory failure
  • Staphylococcal pneumonia or necrotising pneumonia recognised and treated early
Resource-constrained setting
  • Pulse oximetry and oxygen are the two interventions that most change mortality: make sure both exist at every point of care
  • Oral amoxicillin for non-severe pneumonia reduces unnecessary admission and injections
  • Refer early for complications or failure of oxygen, with oxygen for the journey

4 · Prevention Good-to-know

India lens

India introduced pneumococcal conjugate vaccine into the Universal Immunization Programme in phases from 2017, reaching national coverage later; Hib vaccine is part of the pentavalent vaccine. Pneumonia also tracks malnutrition, indoor air pollution (solid-fuel cooking) and poor access to care. The national SAANS campaign (Social Awareness and Action to Neutralise Pneumonia Successfully) focuses on early recognition, pulse oximetry and oxygen at facilities.

1 · Recognise it Must-know

The Second Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (PALICC-2, 2023) defines paediatric ARDS as:

  • onset within 7 days of a known clinical insult;
  • new opacities on imaging consistent with lung-tissue disease;
  • oedema not fully explained by heart failure or fluid overload;
  • hypoxaemia graded by oxygenation index (OI) or oxygen saturation index (OSI) in ventilated children, or by P/F or S/F ratios on non-invasive support.

PALICC-2 also introduced “possible PARDS” and “at risk for PARDS” categories and allows diagnosis where advanced imaging or gases are not available.

Oxygenation index
  OI  = (FiO2 × mean airway pressure × 100) ÷ PaO2
  OSI = (FiO2 × mean airway pressure × 100) ÷ SpO2
  (use OSI when SpO2 is 97% or below and no arterial gas is available)

3 · Adjuncts and fluids Good-to-know PG

3 · Peak, plateau and time Must-know UG/PG

Peak pressure = resistance component + elastic component. Plateau pressure, measured during an inspiratory hold in a passive child, reflects the elastic component.

2 · Displacement and obstruction Must-know

Danger

3 · Pneumothorax and stacked breaths Must-know UG/PG

Pneumothorax causes abrupt deterioration, asymmetric chest movement and air entry, rising pressures and, in tension, falling blood pressure. In a collapsing child it is a clinical diagnosis: a trained clinician should treat suspected tension promptly under the local pathway without waiting for an X-ray. Point-of-care ultrasound can help where there is a trained operator.

Stacked breaths (air trapping) happen when the next breath starts before the last one is out — typically in asthma or bronchiolitis, and often during fast hand-bagging. The chest becomes over-inflated, venous return falls and the child can arrest. Disconnect briefly to let the trapped air out (gentle chest pressure may help), then ventilate at a slower rate.

4 · Follow the gas path Must-know

  • Low-pressure or low-volume alarm: disconnection, leak, displaced tube.
  • High-pressure alarm: obstruction, biting, coughing, bronchospasm, falling compliance, pneumothorax.
  • Check in order: gas source (pipeline, cylinder level), connections, circuit, water in tubing, filters, valves, the ventilator itself.

An alarm names what the machine detected, not the cause. If hand-bagging restores chest movement and SpO₂, suspect the machine or circuit — but reassess the child and tube anyway.

India lens

In many Indian units, oxygen comes from a hospital PSA plant or manifold with shared pressure. A sudden drop in pipeline pressure can affect several ventilated children at once. After stabilisation, record the sequence, suspected cause, what corrected it and the response. Ask whether fixation, a movement checklist, a handover or an alarm setting needs to change. A team drill — one person on the airway, one on the circuit, one preparing manual support, one leading — builds the reflex before it is needed.

1 · How long will the cylinder last? Must-know

  • Oxygen systems. Many Indian district hospitals gained pressure-swing adsorption (PSA) plants during COVID-19. Whether they still work depends on maintenance, spare parts and trained technicians; solar-powered oxygen and concentrator programmes are extending supply to small facilities.
  • Bubble CPAP in low-resource settings. Trials differ: benefit in a Bangladeshi hospital with close physician monitoring, a signal of possible harm in Malawian district hospitals without it, and benefit again in Ethiopian general hospitals (2024) with a dedicated bed space and supervision. Current work focuses on who benefits and what supervision makes it safe (Unit 9).
  • Personalised ventilation. Closed-loop oxygen control, driving-pressure-guided and oesophageal-pressure-guided ventilation, and lung ultrasound are being studied in children; most are not yet standard care.
Checkpoint 1 of 2

Meera (Stage 3) is on bubble CPAP. Her SpO₂ is 91% but she is drowsy with shallow breathing, and her gas shows pH 7.18 and PCO₂ 72 mmHg. The planned CPAP trial has one more hour to run. What should happen?

Nudge

Which failure is present: oxygenation or ventilation?

Structured hint

Drowsiness, shallow breathing and a high CO₂ mean she is not clearing CO₂. Does a planned endpoint matter now?

Why the answer is what it is

Drowsiness with rising CO₂ and acidosis is ventilatory failure, and a reasonable SpO₂ on oxygen is hiding it. Escalate now: call the most experienced airway team and prepare physiology and equipment for intubation.

A — escalate on the trajectory, not on the timetable. B — more oxygen does not fix CO₂ retention and can hide it further. D — sedating a child with ventilatory failure on non-invasive support can cause respiratory arrest.
A good number on a drowsy child is a warning, not reassurance.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

After Meera is repositioned (Stage 4), SpO₂ falls, air entry is louder on the right, and the EtCO₂ waveform is still present. The depth mark has moved from 12 cm to 14 cm at the gums. What is the most likely cause and first step?

Nudge

What does EtCO₂ confirm, and what does it not confirm?

Structured hint

The tube has moved 2 cm deeper and air entry is louder on the right. Where is the tip likely to be?

Why the answer is what it is

EtCO₂ confirms the tube is in the airway, not its depth. A tube that has moved deeper with right-sided air entry is most likely in the right main bronchus. Withdraw to the recorded depth, reassess air entry and SpO₂, and keep pneumothorax in mind if it does not correct.

B — an oesophageal tube does not produce a sustained EtCO₂ waveform. C — the finding is asymmetric air entry after a move, not a machine fault. D — increasing PEEP does not fix a misplaced tube.
CO₂ says ‘in the airway’; only the depth mark and air entry say ‘in the right place’.
Assessment

Final assessment and certificate

Three independent criteria

A single score is not a defensible basis for a high-stakes decision about a clinician. This module certifies on three independent criteria, each measuring something the others cannot.

Where you stand

Coverage asks whether you have met every unit. Retention asks whether the learning survived a night's sleep — it counts breadth across units, not raw items, because a raw count can be satisfied entirely from the units you met first, leaving the newest and most fragile learning untested. Applied performance asks whether you can integrate across units under closed-book conditions.

The closed-book integrated assessment

  • 50 items sampled from a pool of 70 — your 40 unit checkpoints plus 30 fresh integrative items written for this assessment only.
  • Safety rule. About a third of the pool is marked safety-critical — items where the wrong choice could kill a child (a displaced tube, a failing CPAP trial, running out of oxygen on the road). An attempt with any safety-critical miss does not count towards the applied criterion, whatever its score. The review after each attempt shows which items were safety-critical.
  • Item and option order randomised on every attempt, so two attempts are never the same paper and answer-position memory is worthless.
  • 75 minutes, no hints, no rationales, no feedback until you submit. You may move back and forth and change answers.
  • Two attempts, with a 24-hour lock between them — deliberately, so that the gap is filled by remediation and sleep rather than by immediate re-guessing.
  • Cut score 80%. Shipped labelled provisional — see the note below and the standard-setting worksheets in Appendix C.
  • On submission you get a per-unit remediation plan built from your actual misses, and an item-by-item review with full rationales.

—

If you do not reach the cut score

This is designed to be recoverable, not punitive. In order:

  1. Read the remediation plan printed after your attempt. It names the units your misses clustered in, ranked.
  2. Re-open those units and re-answer their checkpoints cold. Do not skim the prose first — attempt the questions, fail them if you are going to, and let the rationale teach you. Retrieval before re-reading is worth several passive re-readings.
  3. Use the hints deliberately. A hinted item stays in the first spaced-retrieval box, so it will come back tomorrow and the day after until you own it.
  4. Work the key-feature problems in Appendix A. They test the same decisions in an open-response format, which exposes gaps that multiple choice hides.
  5. Sleep on it. The 24-hour lock is not an obstacle; it is part of the intervention.

If after two attempts you are still short, ask your faculty to reset your attempts from the panel below. There is no limit on learning, only on how many attempts count toward one certificate.

On the cut score — read this before you defend it to anyone

A cut score chosen by preference is the weakest defensible option available. Published comparisons of standard-setting methods applied to the same assessment have produced cut scores ranging from roughly 66% to 86% — which means the method you choose can matter more than the candidates' performance. This module therefore ships its cut score as provisional, and Appendix C provides modified Angoff, Ebel and Hofstee worksheets plus borderline-regression guidance so your institution can set a defensible one for your own cohort before using this for any consequential decision.

Short answers, no options. They test only the decisions on which the case turns. Use them in remediation clinics and vivas.

KF1 — The quiet child on oxygen

A 4-month-old with bronchiolitis on high-flow for 2 hours: heart rate 180 → 190/min, respiratory rate 70 → 76/min, FiO₂ 0.4 → 0.6, one apnoea.

  1. Success or failure?
    Model: failure — rising rates, rising FiO₂, apnoea.
  2. Next step?
    Model: escalate to CPAP or intubation per local plan; call PICU or arrange transfer now.
KF3 — The transfer

A child on 5 L/min needs a 2-hour road transfer. One 680 L cylinder, full.

  1. How long will it last, and what do you need?
    Model: 680 ÷ 5 = 136 min; plan for 240 min (1200 L), so at least one more full cylinder.
  2. What else must travel with the child?
    Model: self-inflating bag and mask, suction, oximeter, trained escort, written handover, accepting team informed.
KF4 — The ventilated child who worsens

A ventilated 3-year-old is turned; SpO₂ falls to 75%, high-pressure alarm, suction catheter will not pass.

  1. Most likely cause?
    Model: obstruction of the tube (DOPES: O).
  2. What do you do?

Debriefing — the PEARLS structure

Debrief for at least as long as the scenario ran. Sit down. Phones away.

PhaseTimeSay
Setting the scene1 min“We're here to learn, not to judge. What's said here stays here. I believe everyone did their best with the information they had.”
Reactions2 min“In a word or two — how was that?” Let everyone speak. Do not analyse yet.
Description2 min“Can someone walk us through what happened, clinically?” Establish shared facts before opinions.
Analysis10–15 minThe body of the work. Choose 2–3 points, no more. Use the three methods below.
Summary3 min“One thing to keep, one thing to change.” From them, not from you.

Three analysis methods, chosen by what you are addressing:

  • Plus–delta — for performance that was broadly good. “What worked? What would you change?” Fast, safe, low yield.
  • Advocacy–inquiry — for a specific puzzling action. State your observation and your reasoning, then ask theirs: “I noticed compressions started while the chest wasn't moving. I was concerned because that circulates deoxygenated blood. I'm curious what you were seeing at that moment.” This is the highest-yield technique and the hardest to do well — the inquiry must be genuine.
  • Directive feedback with teaching — for a knowledge gap. Do not use inquiry to extract a fact somebody does not have; that is humiliating. Say the fact, explain the mechanism, move on.
Pearl — debrief the system, not only the people

At least one of your two or three analysis points should be about the environment: where the oxygen cylinder and the self-inflating bag were, how long help took, whether the clock was visible. Those findings are the ones that improve the next real resuscitation, including the ones this team is not present for.

C5 · Standard setting — set your own cut score before you use this for anything consequential

Why this matters more than you think

Published comparisons of standard-setting methods applied to the same assessment have produced cut scores ranging from roughly 66% to 86%. That means the method you choose can matter more than how your candidates perform. A cut score chosen by preference — including the 80% this module ships with — is the weakest defensible option available. Do one of the exercises below with three to six judges before the module carries any consequence.

Modified Angoff worksheet
  1. Define the borderline candidate in writing, as a person: “a first-year resident or ward nurse who would reliably recognise respiratory failure, start oxygen to a target and give effective bag-mask breaths, but would hesitate over ventilator settings or lung-protective limits.” Judges must agree this before seeing any item.
  2. For each item, each judge independently estimates: out of 100 borderline candidates, how many would answer this correctly? Record as a percentage.
  3. Discuss items where judges differ by more than 20 points. Do not force consensus; discuss, then re-rate independently.
  4. The cut score is the mean of the judges' mean estimates.
  5. Record the standard error and consider whether to adjust for it. Document everything.
Ebel worksheet

Classify each item on two axes, then assign an expected percentage-correct to each cell.

 EasyMediumHard
Essential—%—%—%
Important—%—%—%
Acceptable—%—%—%

Cut score = Σ(items in cell × expected % for that cell) ÷ total items. Ebel is useful here because the module already tiers content as must / good / nice-to-know, which maps onto essential / important / acceptable.

Hofstee — a sanity check on whatever you produce

Judges agree four values: the minimum and maximum acceptable cut score, and the minimum and maximum acceptable failure rate. Plot the cumulative score distribution and find where the line crosses the rectangle those four values define. If your Angoff or Ebel cut falls outside that rectangle, something is wrong — either with the standard or with the assessment. Hofstee will not give you a defensible cut on its own, but it will stop you shipping an indefensible one.

Borderline regression, for the OSCE

For each station, examiners record both a checklist score and a global rating (fail / borderline / pass / good pass). Regress checklist score on global rating; the cut score is the checklist score predicted by a global rating of “borderline”. This is more defensible than a fixed percentage because it is anchored in expert judgement of these candidates on this station, and it generates the data as a by-product of examining.

G1 · Design decisions and the evidence behind them

DecisionWhy
Mastery, not completion — no “mark as read” control existsCompletion tracking measures exposure, not learning. A control that lets a learner record progress without demonstrating anything corrupts every downstream figure, including the certificate
Two checkpoint items per unit, answered correctly currentlyMastery is a state, not an event. Failing an item later removes the unit's mastered status, which is what makes the coverage criterion meaningful at the moment of certification
Sequential Part unlockingLater content genuinely depends on earlier content — Part C's escalation decisions make no sense without Part A's difference between oxygenation and ventilation. Gating also prevents the common self-paced failure of skimming to the topic that looks most interesting
Spaced retrieval at 1, 3, 7, 21, 60 daysRetrieval practice at expanding intervals produces substantially better long-term retention than re-reading. Interleaving across units rather than blocking by unit further improves discrimination
Confidence rating before each answer; “confident and wrong” flaggedHigh-confidence errors, once corrected with feedback, are corrected more durably than low-confidence ones (the hypercorrection effect; Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001). Rating confidence also exposes miscalibration, which in clinical work is the profile that harms patients. Confidence-based marking in medical schools (Gardner-Medwin) is the educational precedent. The module reports calibration; it does not score it
A review queue with a due count, and a single “next step”The testing effect is robust in medical education (Larsen, Butler & Roediger 2009) and spacing outperforms massing (Cepeda et al. 2006). A visible due count invites the retrieval that is otherwise the least-performed part of a self-paced course; one named next action reduces the decision cost that stalls self-directed learners
Recognition tied to mastery only — no points, badges, leaderboards or streak resetsShort, informative acknowledgement of a mastered unit or an unlocked Part uses the goal-gradient effect without rewarding clicks. Study days are counted but never reset to zero or shamed: learners in Indian postings work rotating nights and irregular duty, and a punitive streak penalises the schedule, not the learning. Gamification reviews in health-professions education report mixed effects, weakest where rewards are decoupled from the learning objective
A failed item returns in 10 minutes at box 1Immediate re-exposure after an error, followed by expanding intervals, is more effective than either immediate repetition alone or long delay
Hinted items stay in box 1A hint changes what the item measured. Advancing the interval would record retrieval strength the learner has not demonstrated
Rationales explain why wrong options are wrongDistractor-level feedback corrects the specific misconception that produced the error; “the answer is B” corrects nothing
Distractors are errors clinicians actually makeImplausible distractors inflate scores and teach nothing. Several items here are built around a specific documented failure mode
Three independent certification criteriaA single score conflates coverage, retention and integration. Separating them lets the certificate state what was actually demonstrated
Retention counts breadth across units, not raw itemsA raw count can be satisfied entirely from units met earliest, leaving the newest and most fragile learning untested
Randomised item and option order; 24-hour lock between attemptsDefeats answer-position memory; the lock forces the gap to be filled with remediation and sleep rather than immediate re-guessing

Dual-track ideal / resource-constrained panels as equalsGuidance written for well-resourced settings is routinely taught unmodified where the equipment does not exist, which teaches learners that correct practice is impossible for them
Emergency dosing never gatedClinical safety material behind a quiz is a patient-safety problem

G3 · Where this design is weaker than it looks

Published honestly, because a module claiming to be evidence-governed must be willing to
  1. Fixed intervals, not fitted forgetting curves. The Leitner schedule (1-3-7-21-60) is a crude approximation. A genuinely adaptive system would fit each learner's forgetting curve per item. This does not, and cannot, without collecting the data it deliberately does not collect. That is a real trade-off, not a free lunch.
  2. Thin item sampling per unit. Two checkpoint items cannot reliably sample a unit's content. Mastery of a unit here means “answered these two specific items correctly”, which is a weaker claim than it appears. The final assessment partially compensates; it does not eliminate the problem.
  3. Rule-based placement, not adaptive testing. The three placement questions set defaults by simple rules. They are not a measurement, and they do not adjust as evidence accumulates about what the learner actually knows.
  4. Limited form-to-form equivalence. Attempts sample 50 items from a pool of 70 with randomised order, so two attempts are not statistically equated. A score of 88% on one attempt is not strictly the same as 88% on another.
  5. Unproctored assessment. Nothing prevents a learner opening the module in another tab, or working with a colleague. The module is honest about this: it is a self-assessment with a certificate attached, and any high-stakes use requires proctoring and the skills assessment in Appendices A and B.
  6. The verification code is not tamper-proof. It is a deterministic hash of the displayed figures, re-derivable by anyone with the same inputs. It helps detect transcription error. It is not a security feature and is described as such on the certificate.
  7. The cut score is provisional and was chosen by preference. Appendix C exists because this is the weakest defensible option. Until your institution does the standard setting, treat the pass/fail line as an approximation.
  8. Kirkpatrick levels 3 and 4 are unmeasured. This module has no evidence yet that it changes bedside respiratory care or child outcomes. Nor does almost any comparable resource — but the absence should be stated rather than implied away.
  9. Accessibility is targeted, not audited. WCAG 2.2 AA conformance has not been independently verified, and has not been tested with real screen-reader users. Automated checks and manual keyboard testing are not the same as a user audit.
  10. Clinical content has not been externally peer reviewed. It is written to the current primary guidelines and cross-checked, but it carries no editorial board and no formal review process. Corrections are welcomed and should be treated as expected rather than exceptional — see CONTRIBUTING.
  11. India-specific epidemiology moves. The figures quoted are from the most recent sources available at build; verify against the current SRS bulletin and national cause-of-death estimates before quoting them in teaching.
  12. One author, one perspective. Every choice about what is must-know versus nice-to-know reflects a single clinician's judgement about what matters at a district hospital at 2 a.m. Reasonable educators would tier some of it differently.
