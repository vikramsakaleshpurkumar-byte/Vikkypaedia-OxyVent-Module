Part A

Foundations: see the child, then the number

Units 1–3 · ~4 hours

Every device in this module exists to fix one of two problems: getting oxygen into the blood, or getting carbon dioxide out. Part A teaches you to tell them apart at the bedside — and to notice when a reassuring number is hiding a failing child. It is open from the first minute.

▸Unit 1 Start with the child Not attempted

A deteriorating child needs support while the cause is found. The first skill in respiratory care is not choosing a device — it is describing what you see so clearly that the next person knows what to do.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Describe a breathing child by alertness, effort, air entry, perfusion and the reliability of any monitor reading, before naming a diagnosis.
  • UG Make a treatment plan testable: an action, an expected response, a named responder and a reassessment point.
  • UG/PG Call for help in a structure that gets the right person, with the right equipment, quickly.
  • PG Explain what an online assessment can and cannot show about procedural competence.
Your role
  • Student. Describe what you see before naming a diagnosis. State the next supervised action and why.
  • Ward nurse. Communicate a change from baseline, the current support and the help you need; use closed-loop confirmation.
  • PICU nurse. Connect the patient trend to device delivery and team response. Identify which observation would change the plan.
  • PG resident. Make the physiological problem explicit, assign immediate actions and define when you will reassess.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Begin with a description Must-know

Before choosing a device, describe five things: alertness, respiratory effort, air entry, pulse and perfusion, and how reliable the monitor reading is. A diagnosis can guide treatment, but a child who is getting worse needs support while the cause is investigated.

Compare “the saturation is 96%” with “96% on 2 L/min, but he is drowsier and breathing more shallowly than an hour ago”. The second sentence exposes a possible failure of ventilation that the first one hides.

Pearl

Always say what support the number was measured on. 96% in room air and 96% on a non-rebreathing mask are different children.

2 · Make the plan testable Must-know

State the concern, take an action within your role, then look for a specific response. “Will reassess” is weaker than “I have started oxygen at 1 L/min by prongs; I expect SpO₂ at or above 92% and less recession within 15 minutes; if not, I will call the registrar.” Any deterioration triggers review immediately, not at the planned time.

A testable plan
  CONCERN    what is threatened: oxygenation, ventilation, or both?
  ACTION     what you will do, within your role
  EXPECTED   what improvement should look like, and by when
  OWNER      who reassesses, and who responds if it fails
  FAILURE    the finding that makes you escalate NOW

3 · Ask for help that arrives useful Must-know

A ward nurse finds a previously alert child hard to rouse on oxygen. A useful call gives who and where, the worsening trend, current support, what has been done and what help is needed. The receiver repeats back the urgent action and says who is coming.

Ideal setting
  • A rapid response or PICU outreach team with a defined call number and a response time
  • The caller stays with the child and continues support while help arrives
Resource-constrained setting
  • Early escalation also buys time to arrange oxygen for the journey, monitoring, an escort and a receiving team
  • The local clinician remains responsible for immediate stabilisation while transfer is organised
  • Write the call-out number for the referral centre on the oxygen trolley, not in a drawer

4 · What an online result means — and does not Good-to-know UG/PG

A correct first answer is evidence of what you could decide before feedback. A correct repeat after an explanation is evidence of learning with help. Both are useful; neither demonstrates hands-on competence. Bag-mask ventilation, CPAP set-up and airway skills need observation and feedback from a trained assessor on a mannequin and then at the bedside.

Why you rate your confidence

Confidence matters here. Being very sure of a wrong decision is the most dangerous pattern in respiratory care, which is why this module asks how sure you are before every checkpoint and flags “confident and wrong”.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

A 3-year-old with pneumonia has SpO₂ 95% on 2 L/min nasal prongs. An hour ago she was talking; now she is sleepy, her breathing is shallower and her respiratory rate has fallen from 52 to 30/min. The handover says “sats fine on 2 litres”. What is the most accurate description of her state?

Nudge

What does oxygen do to the saturation of a child who is breathing less?

Structured hint

Put the three changes together — sleepier, shallower, slower — and ask whether a normal SpO₂ on oxygen can exclude a rising CO₂.

Why the answer is what it is

Drowsiness with shallower, slower breathing after a period of distress suggests exhaustion and hypoventilation. Supplemental oxygen can keep the saturation normal while CO₂ rises, so the number is falsely reassuring. She needs urgent senior review, airway and breathing support as needed, and a CO₂ or blood-gas assessment.

B — a falling rate with falling consciousness is loss of compensation, not recovery. C — attributing a change in consciousness to tiredness is the classic error. D — she is not hypoxaemic; more oxygen treats the wrong problem and delays escalation.
Always hand over the child's trend and the support she is on, not only the best number of the shift.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

Which of these plans for a 10-month-old with bronchiolitis started on oxygen is the most testable?

Nudge

Which plan tells the next person exactly what success looks like and what to do if it does not happen?

Structured hint

Look for four parts: an action, a target, a named person and time to reassess, and a failure trigger.

Why the answer is what it is

A testable plan has an action, an expected response, an owner and time for reassessment, and a failure trigger. Only option C has all four.

A — no target, no time, no failure plan. B — “sats up” has no number and no escalation. D — a fixed high flow without reassessment can hide deterioration and wastes oxygen.
If the night nurse cannot tell from your plan when to call you, it is not a plan.

▸Unit 2 Recognise distress and failure Not attempted

Respiratory distress is a child working hard and winning. Respiratory failure is a child working hard and losing — or no longer able to work at all. The difference is often quiet.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Recognise signs of increased work of breathing and interpret the respiratory rate for age.
  • UG Recognise the signs of respiratory failure, including the tiring child whose rate is falling.
  • UG/PG Localise the problem — upper airway, lower airway, lung tissue, or control of breathing — without letting it delay support.
  • PG Recognise mixed failure: hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and circulatory failure together.
Your role
  • Student. Describe the pattern and the physiological threat separately, and justify the urgency of your review.
  • Ward nurse. Escalate falling consciousness, weaker effort or worse air entry even if an early-warning score looks reassuring.
  • PICU nurse. Compare support needs, waveform quality and examination; recognise deterioration before profound desaturation.
  • PG resident. State whether oxygenation, ventilation or both are threatened, and coordinate support with cause-directed evaluation.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Distress can still be compensation Must-know

Fast breathing, recession, nasal flaring, head bobbing and grunting show increased work. An alert child with good air entry may still be maintaining gas exchange — but distress can progress. Interpret the rate against age, temperature, pain and the child's own baseline.

AgeFast breathing (WHO/IMNCI)
Under 2 months60/min or more
2–11 months50/min or more
1–5 years40/min or more

Do not reduce severity to one sign. Ask whether the effort is effective: is air moving, can the child interact or feed, is circulation maintained? Reassess after every intervention and record the direction of change.

2 · Less noise is not improvement Must-know

The tiring child

A previously distressed child who becomes quiet, weak, drowsy or apnoeic may be exhausting. Poor air entry, reduced effort and bradycardia are dangerous findings. A normal saturation on oxygen cannot rule this out. Call skilled help and support breathing now — do not wait for a blood gas to prove failure.

Contrast true recovery: easier breathing, better interaction and feeding, better air entry, stable circulation and less support needed.

3 · Locate the pattern without over-claiming Must-know

PatternCluesThink of

The error clinicians actually make

The silent chest. In severe asthma a nearly silent chest means very little air is moving — not that the bronchospasm has resolved. Grunting is an attempt to hold lung volume open. Read every sign in the whole-child picture, and never let localisation delay stabilisation.

4 · Mixed failure Good-to-know UG/PG

Hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and circulatory failure often coexist. A child can need oxygen, ventilation support and treatment of poor perfusion at the same time. When the response to one intervention is incomplete, ask which other mechanism is still unaddressed.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

A 4-year-old with severe wheeze was alert with RR 48/min and marked recession. Thirty minutes later RR is 24/min, she is drowsy, her speech is weaker and air entry is reduced. SpO₂ is 93% on a non-rebreathing mask. What is happening?

Nudge

What else changed at the same time as the respiratory rate?

Structured hint

Recovery brings easier breathing and better interaction. Here consciousness and air entry fell with the rate. What does that combination mean?

Why the answer is what it is

A falling rate with falling consciousness, weaker speech and reduced air entry is loss of compensation — exhaustion. Call for help, support breathing and prepare escalation. Hand over the paired observations so “RR improved” is not mistaken for recovery.

A — the rate fell for the wrong reason. B — reduced air entry is a quieter chest, which in severe asthma is a danger sign. C — salbutamol causes tachycardia and tremor, not drowsiness with poor air entry.
When a struggling child goes quiet, find out why before you relax.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 7-month-old has noisy breathing. Which finding most strongly suggests the problem is in the upper airway?

Nudge

Which phase of breathing makes noise when the narrowing is above the chest?

Structured hint

Upper-airway narrowing is worst when the airway is sucked in during inspiration. Which option describes an inspiratory noise?

Why the answer is what it is

Inspiratory stridor with a hoarse cry points to narrowing at or near the larynx — an upper-airway pattern.

A — crackles suggest lung-tissue disease. B — expiratory wheeze suggests lower-airway obstruction. D — irregular breathing with little effort suggests a problem with the control of breathing.
Localise, then support. The pattern tells you which device and which danger to expect.

▸Unit 3 Oxygenation is not ventilation Not attempted

Oxygenation puts oxygen into blood; ventilation takes carbon dioxide out. They fail for different reasons, respond to different treatments, and — most dangerously — a pulse oximeter only watches one of them.

Learning outcomes
  • UG Explain the difference between oxygenation and ventilation, and why supplemental oxygen can hide hypoventilation.
  • UG Explain why saturation is a proportion, and why an anaemic or shocked child can be well saturated but poorly oxygenated.
  • UG/PG Name the four mechanisms of hypoxaemia and predict which respond to oxygen.
  • PG Use minute and alveolar ventilation to explain why rapid shallow breathing clears little CO₂.
Your role
  • Student. Explain the oxygen-delivery chain from lungs to haemoglobin to cardiac output.
  • Ward nurse. Recognise when a satisfactory saturation does not match the child's state, and request review.
  • PICU nurse. Integrate blood-gas and CO₂ trends with support, perfusion and oxygenation.
  • PG resident. Identify the mechanism limiting delivery or ventilation and choose the next investigation without delaying support.

Role notes say what to emphasise. They do not authorise any procedure beyond your training and local policy.

1 · Two different jobs Must-know

Oxygenation transfers oxygen into blood. Ventilation clears carbon dioxide. Alveolar ventilation depends on useful gas reaching gas-exchanging lung, not on the number of breaths. Rapid shallow breaths waste much of each breath in dead space.

Supplemental oxygen can maintain SpO₂ despite falling ventilation. If breathing is shallow or consciousness worsens, ask about CO₂ clearance: a normal saturation cannot answer that question.

Minute and alveolar ventilation
  minute ventilation   = tidal volume × rate
  alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume − dead space) × rate

  Child A: 150 mL × 20/min = 3.0 L/min   dead space 50 mL
           alveolar = (150 − 50) × 20 = 2.0 L/min
  Child B:  75 mL × 40/min = 3.0 L/min   dead space 50 mL
           alveolar = (75 − 50) × 40 = 1.0 L/min
  Same minute ventilation. Child B clears half the CO2.

2 · Saturation is a proportion, not a quantity Must-know

Most oxygen in blood is carried on haemoglobin. Saturation is the proportion of that carrier which is occupied. A severely anaemic child can have 98% saturation and still too little oxygen content. Tissue delivery also depends on cardiac output: well-saturated blood that does not flow does not oxygenate organs.

Comfortable, in room airWell
Severe anaemia (Hb 4 g/dL), poor perfusionLow oxygen content and delivery — needs blood and circulatory support
Drowsy, shallow breathing, on oxygenPossible hypoventilation masked by oxygen — needs CO₂ assessment and breathing support

3 · Why the same oxygen has different effects Must-know UG/PG

Low inspired oxygenAltitudeYes
HypoventilationOpioid, exhaustion, neuromuscular weaknessSaturation improves, but CO₂ keeps rising — the real treatment is ventilation
Ventilation/perfusion mismatchPneumonia, bronchiolitis, asthmaUsually yes
ShuntCollapsed or flooded lung, cyanotic heart diseasePoorly — needs recruitment (PEEP/CPAP) or treatment of the cause
Pearl

If a child needs more and more oxygen for less and less effect, think shunt: the lung needs opening (CPAP, PEEP), not just a higher FiO₂.

4 · The curve is a warning about reserve Good-to-know

The oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve is flat at high oxygen tensions and steep lower down. Once a child reaches the steep part, a small fall in oxygen tension produces a fast fall in saturation. That is why a child “holding” at 90–92% can desaturate abruptly — watch trends and reserve, not a single number. The curve's position shifts with temperature, pH and CO₂; it is not a calculator for converting an unreliable SpO₂ reading.

Checkpoint 1 of 2

Three children each have SpO₂ 98%. Which one is most likely to have inadequate oxygen delivery to the tissues despite this reading?

Nudge

What else, apart from saturation, determines how much oxygen reaches the organs?

Structured hint

Oxygen delivery depends on saturation, haemoglobin and cardiac output. Which child is short of the other two?

Why the answer is what it is

Oxygen delivery depends on saturation × haemoglobin × cardiac output. The child with Hb 4 g/dL and poor perfusion has a normal saturation but little carrier and little flow — tissue oxygen delivery is poor.

B, C and D — each has adequate haemoglobin and circulation; the saturation reflects their state reasonably.
A saturation tells you how full the lorries are, not how many lorries there are or whether they are moving.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

A 9-year-old with Guillain–Barré syndrome has shallow breathing. On 2 L/min oxygen his SpO₂ is 97%, but he is becoming drowsy. What is the main danger the saturation is not showing?

Nudge

Which respiratory job fails first when the muscles are weak?

Structured hint

Oxygen can keep the saturation normal. What gas builds up when breathing is shallow, and what does it do to consciousness?

Why the answer is what it is

Weak, shallow breathing causes hypoventilation: CO₂ rises and consciousness falls, while supplemental oxygen keeps the saturation normal. He needs CO₂ assessment and ventilatory support, not reassurance from the oximeter.

A — his saturation is normal on oxygen; hypoxaemia is not the problem shown. B — 2 L/min is not toxic; the danger is what the oxygen hides. C — nothing suggests anaemia.
In neuromuscular weakness, watch the effort and the CO₂, not the saturation.
